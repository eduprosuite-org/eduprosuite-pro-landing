1. Parameters vs. Statistics & Sampling Distribution Foundations

A parameter describes a numerical characteristic of an entire population ($\mu$, $\sigma$, $p$), whereas a statistic describes a numerical characteristic calculated from a sample ($\bar{x}$, $s$, $\hat{p}$). A sampling distribution is the probability distribution of a statistic obtained through a large number of samples drawn from a specific population. Mastering sampling distributions is essential for deriving confidence intervals and executing inference within the hypothesis testing framework.

2. The Central Limit Theorem (CLT)

The Central Limit Theorem is one of the most powerful theorems in all of mathematics. It states:

💡 Official College Board Definition: When taking random samples of size $n$ from ANY population with mean $\mu$ and standard deviation $\sigma$ (regardless of population shape—skewed, bimodal, or uniform), the sampling distribution of the sample mean $\bar{x}$ approaches an approximately normal distribution as $n$ increases ($n \ge 30$).
⚠️ AP Exam Misconception Trap: The CLT applies ONLY to sample means ($\bar{x}$), NOT to sample proportions ($\hat{p}$). For sample proportions, approximate normality is established by the Large Counts Condition ($np \ge 10$ and $n(1-p) \ge 10$), not the Central Limit Theorem!

3. Mathematical Formulas for Means and Proportions

From the official AP Statistics formula sheet, the mean and standard error of sampling distributions are defined as:

Statistic Type Mean ($\mu_{\text{statistic}}$) Standard Error ($\sigma_{\text{statistic}}$) Normality Condition
Sample Proportion ($\hat{p}$) $\mu_{\hat{p}} = p$ $\sigma_{\hat{p}} = \sqrt{\frac{p(1-p)}{n}}$ $np \ge 10$ and $n(1-p) \ge 10$
Sample Mean ($\bar{x}$) $\mu_{\bar{x}} = \mu$ $\sigma_{\bar{x}} = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}$ Population normal OR $n \ge 30$ (CLT)

4. The 10% Independence Rule

When sampling without replacement from a finite population, observations are technically dependent. However, as long as the sample size $n$ is less than 10% of the population size $N$ ($n < 0.10N$), the dependence is negligible, and the formula for standard deviation remains valid. Frequently Asked Questions

❓ Does increasing sample size decrease bias?

No! Increasing sample size decreases variability (spread), but has zero effect on bias. A large biased sample simply gives a very precise incorrect estimate.
