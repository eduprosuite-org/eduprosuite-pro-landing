1. Area Between Curves: Top-Minus-Bottom vs. Right-Minus-Left

Finding the area bounded between intersecting curves requires integrating the differential difference between the upper and lower boundary curves (or right and left curves for $y$-integration):

$$A = \int_a^b [f(x) - g(x)]\,dx \quad \text{where } f(x) \ge g(x) \quad \text{or} \quad A = \int_c^d [f(y) - g(y)]\,dy \quad \text{where } f(y) \ge g(y)$$

To determine the integration limits $a$ and $b$, set $f(x) = g(x)$ and solve for the intersection points. If the curves cross within the interval, partition the integral at the intersection points so that the integrand remains strictly positive. Compare these integration techniques with our comprehensive integral rules guide.

2. Disk Method: Solid Revolutions Around Axes

When a planar region is revolved around an axis of revolution that directly bounds the region (creating a solid with no inner hollow cavity), the cross-sections perpendicular to the axis are circular disks with radius $R(x)$:

$$V = \pi \int_a^b [R(x)]^2\,dx \quad (\text{Horizontal Axis}) \qquad V = \pi \int_c^d [R(y)]^2\,dy \quad (\text{Vertical Axis})$$

Each differential slice represents a circular cylinder of volume $dV = \pi R^2 dx$. When writing these integrals on the exam, ensure the constant $\pi$ is placed outside the integral to prevent arithmetic mistakes. Review this on the AP Calculus formulas sheet.

3. Washer Method & Shifted Rotation Axes ($y = k$ & $x = h$)

When there is a space between the bounded region and the axis of revolution, rotating the region produces a hollow inner core. The cross-sections are washers with an outer radius $R(x)$ and an inner radius $r(x)$:

$$V = \pi \int_a^b \left( [R(x)]^2 - [r(x)]^2 \right)\,dx$$
⚠️ Fatal Student Trap Alert: Never square the difference! The integral is $\pi \int (R^2 - r^2)\,dx$, NOT $\pi \int (R - r)^2\,dx$. Writing $(R - r)^2$ is an automatic zero on that scoring rubric point!
💡 Worked Example (Shifted Axis $y = -1$): Find volume when region bounded by $y = \sqrt{x}$, $y = 0$, and $x = 4$ is revolved around the line $y = -1$.
1. Axis is horizontal: Integrate with respect to $x$ from $x = 0$ to $x = 4$.
2. Outer radius $R(x)$: Distance from axis $y = -1$ to top curve $y = \sqrt{x} \implies R(x) = \sqrt{x} - (-1) = \sqrt{x} + 1$.
3. Inner radius $r(x)$: Distance from axis $y = -1$ to bottom curve $y = 0 \implies r(x) = 0 - (-1) = 1$.
4. Setup Integral: $V = \pi \int_0^4 \left( (\sqrt{x} + 1)^2 - (1)^2 \right)\,dx = \pi \int_0^4 (x + 2\sqrt{x} + 1 - 1)\,dx$.
5. Evaluate: $\pi \left[ \frac{1}{2}x^2 + \frac{4}{3}x^{3/2} \right]_0^4 = \pi \left( 8 + \frac{32}{3} \right) = \frac{56\pi}{3}$.

4. Volumes with Known Cross-Sections (Squares, Semicircles, Triangles)

Unlike solids of revolution, solids with known cross-sections are formed by building geometric shapes perpendicularly upon a two-dimensional base region. The volume is calculated by integrating the cross-sectional area function $A(x)$ or $A(y)$:

$$V = \int_a^b A(x)\,dx$$

Let the base length of the cross-section be $b(x) = \text{Top Curve} - \text{Bottom Curve} = f(x) - g(x)$. The area formulas based on geometry are:

Cross-Section Shape Area Formula $A(x)$ Integral Formulation
Squares $A = b^2$ $V = \int_a^b [f(x) - g(x)]^2\,dx$
Semicircles $A = \frac{1}{2}\pi r^2 = \frac{1}{8}\pi b^2$ $V = \frac{\pi}{8} \int_a^b [f(x) - g(x)]^2\,dx$
Equilateral Triangles $A = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{4} b^2$ $V = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{4} \int_a^b [f(x) - g(x)]^2\,dx$
Isosceles Right Triangles (Leg on base) $A = \frac{1}{2} b^2$ $V = \frac{1}{2} \int_a^b [f(x) - g(x)]^2\,dx$
Isosceles Right Triangles (Hypotenuse on base) $A = \frac{1}{4} b^2$ $V = \frac{1}{4} \int_a^b [f(x) - g(x)]^2\,dx$

Notice that the geometric multiplier can always be factored outside the integral. Explore more geometric derivations in our Calculus dictionary.

5. Arc Length & Surface Area Integrals (AP® Calculus BC)

For smooth functions on $[a, b]$, the arc length of the curve is derived from the Pythagorean theorem on infinitesimal differentials $ds = \sqrt{dx^2 + dy^2}$:

$$L = \int_a^b \sqrt{1 + [f'(x)]^2}\,dx \qquad \text{or} \qquad L = \int_c^d \sqrt{1 + [g'(y)]^2}\,dy$$

Mastering these derivations equips you for polar and parametric curve lengths covered in our parametric and polar calculus guide.

Frequently Asked Questions (AP® Calculus AB & BC)

Q: How do I know whether to integrate with respect to x or y?

For cross-sections perpendicular to the x-axis, integrate dx. For cross-sections perpendicular to the y-axis, integrate dy. For revolutions: if the axis is horizontal (like y = 2), use dx for disks/washers. If the axis is vertical (like x = -3), use dy for disks/washers.

Q: Can I use the cylindrical shell method on the AP Calculus Exam?

Yes! While the College Board curriculum does not require the shell method (all AP exam questions can be solved using disks/washers), grading rubrics explicitly accept correct shell method setups and evaluations.

Q: Where does the 1/8 factor come from in the semicircle cross-section formula?

A semicircle has area A = (1/2)*pi*r^2. The base b represents the full diameter, so radius r = b/2. Substituting gives A = (1/2)*pi*(b/2)^2 = (1/2)*pi*(b^2 / 4) = (pi/8)*b^2.
