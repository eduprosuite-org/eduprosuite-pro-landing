1. Observational Studies vs. Randomized Experiments

The single most critical concept in statistical data collection is establishing when a conclusion of causation is justified:

  • Observational Study: Researchers observe individuals and record variables of interest without attempting to influence responses. Observational studies can show correlation/association, but NEVER causation due to potential confounding variables.
  • Randomized Experiment: Researchers deliberately impose treatments on experimental units and measure response variables. A well-designed randomized experiment is the ONLY study type capable of establishing cause-and-effect conclusions.

When reporting results, reference appropriate inference frameworks in our hypothesis testing guide and estimate treatment impact with confidence intervals.

2. The 4 Fundamental Principles of Experimental Design

To score full credit on AP Statistics experimental design FRQs, you must state and implement all four principles:

  1. Comparison: Use a design that compares two or more treatments (e.g., active treatment vs. control or placebo).
  2. Random Assignment: Use chance to allocate experimental units to treatments. This creates roughly equivalent treatment groups and balances the effects of confounding variables.
  3. Control: Keep other variables that might affect the response the same across all treatment groups to minimize variation in responses.
  4. Replication: Apply each treatment to enough experimental units so that any difference in effects can be distinguished from chance variation.
⚠️ AP Exam Scoring Trap: Replication does NOT mean repeating the entire experiment! Replication means using enough experimental units within each treatment group in a single experiment.

3. Sampling Methods & Systematic Bias

Understanding sampling techniques prevents systematic underrepresentation:

Sampling Method How It Works Primary Advantage
Simple Random Sample (SRS) Every group of $n$ individuals in the population has an equal chance of being selected. Completely unbiased selection.
Stratified Random Sample Classify population into homogeneous groups (strata), then choose an SRS from each stratum. Guarantees representation and reduces variability.
Cluster Sample Divide population into heterogeneous groups (clusters) located near each other, randomly select whole clusters, and survey all individuals within chosen clusters. Cost-effective and practical.
Systematic Sample Select every $k$-th individual after a random start. Easy to implement in field testing.

Be prepared to describe common biases: Undercoverage (certain groups left out of sampling frame), Nonresponse Bias (selected individuals cannot be contacted or refuse to participate), and Response Bias (wording of questions influences answers).

4. Randomized Block & Matched Pairs Designs

A block is a group of experimental units known before the experiment to be similar in some way that is expected to affect the response to treatments (e.g., age, gender, soil type). Blocking reduces unexplained variation in the response variable. "Control what you can, block what you can't control, and randomize the rest."

💡 Matched Pairs Design: A specialized form of blocking where blocks consist of pairs of closely matched units (e.g., identical twins, pre-test and post-test on the same subject), or where each subject receives BOTH treatments in randomized order.

Evaluate sample evidence using the p-value framework and guard against Type I and Type II errors. Review all formulas on the AP Statistics formula sheet and practice with full diagnostic exams in our AP Statistics mock test simulator.

Frequently Asked Questions

❓ What is the purpose of a placebo in a medical experiment?

A placebo provides a baseline comparison and controls for the placebo effect, where patients improve simply because they believe they are receiving treatment.
