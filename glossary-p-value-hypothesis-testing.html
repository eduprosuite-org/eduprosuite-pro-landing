Verified Educational Definition

P-Value in Hypothesis Testing: Definition, Calculation & Misconceptions

📅 Verified for 2026–2027 Examination Blueprint (Latest CED Edition)
A p-value is the conditional probability of obtaining a test statistic at least as extreme as the observed sample result, assuming the null hypothesis ($H_0$) is strictly true: $P(\text{Data as extreme or more} \mid H_0 \text{ is true})$.

p-value > α (e.g., 0.05)Fail to Reject Null ($H_0$)Insufficient evidence to conclude $H_a$. Never 'accept' $H_0$.
⚠️ Key Examination Standard & Pitfall Prevention:

The 'Inverse Fallacy': The p-value is NOT the probability that the null hypothesis is true, nor is $1 - p$ the probability that the alternative hypothesis is true.

📝 Real-World Exam Case Study & Solution:

A school claims average SAT score μ = 1200. A sample of $n = 64$ students shows $\bar{x} = 1240$ with $s = 100$. The test statistic is $t = \frac{1240 - 1200}{100 / \sqrt{64}} = 3.20$. For $df = 63$, the two-sided p-value is $0.0022$. Since $0.0022 < 0.05$, we reject $H_0$ and conclude the true mean SAT score exceeds 1200.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can a p-value ever equal zero?

No. In continuous probability distributions (like Gaussian or Student's t), probabilities are calculated over intervals; while a p-value can be infinitesimally small (e.g., $10^{-15}$), it is never mathematically zero.

Why do statisticians say 'fail to reject' instead of 'accept'?

Absence of evidence is not evidence of absence. A high p-value simply indicates the data is consistent with $H_0$, not that $H_0$ is proved true.
